# Issue #145 — Pigeon Compound-Applicability Research

> **Purpose and boundary.** This follow-up evaluates whether compounds identified as concerns in other bird evidence can resolve the pigeon outcome for five **raw dried** bean forms. It does not transfer a parrot or chicken suitability outcome to pigeons. A compound may support a pigeon conclusion only where pigeon/columbiform evidence or an explicitly defensible pigeon-applicability basis is documented.

## Source-verified mapping findings

David et al. classify pinto and black beans as dry common beans (*Phaseolus vulgaris*) and fava/broad beans as *Vicia faba*; the same overview separately lists lima bean as *Phaseolus lunatus*. This taxonomy is material to the review: it prevents an outcome for one bean species from being treated as proof for another.[1]

Zhang et al.’s systematic kidney-bean review identifies lectin, trypsin inhibitor, phytic acid, tannin, and saponin as principal non-nutritional factors for *P. vulgaris* beans. It explains their broad mechanisms—lectin and trypsin-inhibitor interference with digestion, phytate chelation of minerals/proteins, and tannin effects on nutrient use—but it does **not** study pigeons, black/pinto/navy cultivars separately, or any pigeon dose/outcome.[2]

The initial map is therefore a search framework, not a pigeon hazard conclusion. The next research stage will seek direct pigeon/columbiform exposure, digestive, haematologic, or toxicology evidence for each candidate compound and bean form.

## Source-specific compound evidence

The source-specific lima-bean review identifies *P. lunatus* antinutrients as including phytohaemagglutinins/lectins, trypsin inhibitors, tannins, oxalate, phytic acid, and cyanide-producing glucosides. It also emphasizes cultivar, maturity, and environmental variation. This supports the lima compound search list but does not supply pigeon evidence, a pigeon dose, or a pigeon outcome.[3]

The faba-bean hen study identifies vicine and convicine as fava-specific glucopyranosides. It states that, after hydrolysis by bean beta-glucosidase or animal intestinal microflora, their derivatives divicine and isouramil are redox-active. In the study’s laying-hen diets, higher V+C was associated with red-blood-cell haemolysis and lower reduced glutathione. This is direct chicken evidence for a compound-specific concern, not evidence that the same effect occurs in pigeons.[4]

## Pigeon and columbiform compound-applicability search

### Fava vicine/convicine and common-bean lectin/trypsin-inhibitor searches

Renewed searches used `Columba livia vicine convicine faba bean`, `pigeon faba bean vicine convicine toxicity`, `pigeon favism vicine convicine`, `Columba livia phytohaemagglutinin lectin bean`, `pigeon trypsin inhibitor legume feeding`, and `pigeon Phaseolus vulgaris lectin toxicity`.

No accessible pigeon or columbiform feeding, toxicology, red-cell, intestinal, pancreatic, or clinical study was returned for vicine/convicine, phytohaemagglutinin/lectins, or trypsin inhibitors from any of the five bean forms. The returned sources were human, rat, poultry, laboratory-injection, social-media, or general compound sources. They do not establish a pigeon outcome and are not used to change a pigeon row.

### Cyanogenic-glycoside search: direct pigeon metabolic context, not a feeding outcome

A search using `Columba livia linamarin cyanogenic glycoside`, `pigeon lima bean cyanide toxicity`, and `pigeon cyanogenic glycoside feeding study` located two direct pigeon enzyme-distribution studies:

- Baghshani and Aminlari measured rhodanese in adult pigeon tissues and found the highest activity in liver, kidney, and heart. The authors describe rhodanese as participating in cyanide detoxification, but the study is an enzyme-distribution study, not a cyanide-exposure or lima-bean feeding study.[5]
- Agboola, Fagbohunka, and Adenuga measured rhodanese and 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase in local pigeons and other birds. Both enzymes were present in pigeon tissues; pigeon rhodanese activity was highest in kidney, while 3-MST was highest in liver. Again, the work provides biochemical context only: it reports neither a lima-bean dose nor a pigeon cyanide tolerance or safety threshold.[6]

The enzyme findings show that pigeons possess these cyanide-related metabolic enzymes. They **cannot** be read as evidence that a pigeon safely detoxifies cyanide released from raw dried lima beans: no exposure amount, rate of cyanide release, dietary sulfur status, clinical outcome, or *P. lunatus* feeding result was tested.

### Phytate/tannin context

Ji et al. report a *Columba livia* complete-diet study in which cereal phosphorus was described as largely phytate-bound and not biologically available; the investigators varied supplemental non-phytate phosphorus and measured adult-pigeon and squab performance/bone outcomes. This is pigeon-specific nutritional context, but it does not measure bean phytate, tannin, raw-bean exposure, or a pigeon threshold for either compound. It does not resolve any bean/form outcome.[7]

## Required targeted follow-up searches

### Fava vicine/convicine

The second targeted search used `Columba livia faba bean vicine erythrocyte hemolysis`, `pigeon broad bean vicine convicine feeding trial`, and `pigeon Vicia faba red blood cells study`. It returned the previously reviewed laying-hen paper and human/rat/general fava records, but no accessible pigeon or columbiform study. A historical paper on species erythrocyte interactions with seed agglutinins was located, but its accessible HTML did not supply results sufficient to link a named fava compound to pigeon dietary exposure; it is not used as outcome evidence.

### Common-bean lectins and trypsin inhibitors

The second targeted search used `pigeon raw bean lectin avian veterinarian`, `Columba livia Phaseolus vulgaris trypsin inhibitor feeding`, and `pigeon phytohaemagglutinin oral toxicity`. Results were social posts, general/other-species compound guidance, unrelated PHA immune-test literature, and general pigeon diet pages. No accessible, citable pigeon/columbiform dietary study or exotics-vet source resolved a raw black, pinto, or navy bean outcome. None is elevated to evidence.

### Cyanogenic glycosides

The second targeted search used `Columba livia oral cyanide toxicity dose`, `pigeon cyanide poisoning experimental study`, and `pigeon cyanogenic plant poisoning veterinary`. It located the citation for Brasel, Cooper, and Pritsos (2006), *Effects of Environmentally Relevant Doses of Cyanide on Flight Times in Pigeons, Columba livia*.[8] The accessible publisher page confirms the direct pigeon/cyanide study title and citation but does not provide its methods, dose, or results. PDF delivery endpoints returned access-gate HTML rather than the paper. Therefore this citation is recorded as a lead only and is **not** used to assert a pigeon cyanide threshold or an outcome. The already verified direct pigeon enzyme studies remain the only usable pigeon cyanide evidence in this review.

## Provisional assessment before ledger decision

The research establishes that the concern compounds exist in the named bean groups and that pigeon-specific metabolic/nutrition context exists for cyanide-related enzymes and phytate-bound phosphorus. It does **not** establish a raw-dried-bean exposure outcome in pigeons. The crucial missing bridge is compound concentration in the specific bean lot/form combined with a pigeon oral-exposure outcome or a well-supported pigeon-specific toxicology threshold. No other bird’s observed outcome is transferred across that gap.
