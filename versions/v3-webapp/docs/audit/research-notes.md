# Research Notes: Nutrition and Safety Audit

## Companion birds and seed-based diets

The MSD Veterinary Manual states that seeds should not comprise most of a companion bird’s diet because they are high in fat and are not sufficiently nutritious. It describes pellets or crumbles as formulated mixtures that include added vitamins and minerals, supplemented by small amounts of fresh vegetables and fruit.[1]

The MSD Veterinary Manual further states that seed-based diets have calcium-to-phosphorus imbalance and amino-acid deficiencies. It identifies all-seed diets as deficient in vitamin A and notes that African grey parrots are prone to acute hypocalcemia associated with poor calcium and vitamin D3 status.[2]

The resulting product implication is that, for parrots, African greys, budgies, and canaries, this application should be explicitly framed as a **seed-treat, enrichment, or supplemental-mix calculator**, not a complete-diet calculator. It should not present protein/carbohydrate/fat/fiber percentages as sufficient proof of nutritional completeness because the current data model does not represent amino acids, calcium, phosphorus, vitamin D3, vitamin A, iodine, trace minerals, or energy density.

## Chickens and complete layer rations

Oregon State University Extension describes manufactured poultry feed as a complete diet. For laying hens, it reports approximately 16% protein and 3–4% calcium; scratch grain is an optional enrichment feed that should be supplied sparingly, around 10–15% of daily consumption, rather than used as the main ration.[3]

Alabama Extension similarly advises that an appropriate complete layer feed should be the sole nutrient source for most small flocks. Its reference composition includes at least 16% protein, 3–4% calcium, and 0.4–0.5% phosphorus. It warns that homemade feeds require in-depth nutritional knowledge and recommends consultation with a poultry-nutrition professional before adopting one.[4]

Mississippi State University Extension adds that whole grains or feeds deficient in minerals and vitamins can contribute to poor eggshell quality and that raw soybeans must be processed before feeding because of antinutritional factors.[5]

The product implication is that chicken mode must be reframed as a **scratch-grain or supplement mix calculator**, not a complete layer-feed formulation tool. A chicken egg-laying mode cannot responsibly claim to optimize a complete diet without at least calcium, phosphorus, amino acids, vitamins, and energy data plus professionally formulated constraints.

## Food hazards and raw legumes

The MSD Veterinary Manual identifies avocado as hazardous to caged birds and documents marked sensitivity in budgerigars and canaries. It also identifies chocolate’s theobromine and caffeine as toxic components.[6]

The University of Georgia explains that raw soybeans contain antinutritional factors; adequate heat processing is necessary to inactivate them, and both under- and over-processing can reduce nutritional quality.[7]

A 2024 poultry review concludes that grain legumes have antinutritional factors and that their nutritional value, digestible amino acids, energy, cultivar, inclusion rate, and processing method all matter. A binary “safe raw / unsafe raw” list is therefore too coarse for an optimization product.[8]

The product implication is that every ingredient needs an explicit processing status and a traceable source. Raw soybean should be blocked rather than merely treated as an ordinary high-protein ingredient. Broad “fatal” labels and fixed boiling instructions should be replaced with concise warnings that direct the user to a qualified avian or poultry-nutrition professional when the ingredient or processing status is uncertain.

## Pigeons and doves

VCA Animal Hospitals notes that pigeons and doves require carbohydrates, protein, fat, vitamins, minerals, and water. It recommends that pellets ideally account for approximately half of the diet, with small amounts of seed and fresh produce; it also says seeds are deficient in calcium, vitamin A, and other nutrients.[9]

Modern pigeon studies formulate and evaluate diets using metabolizable energy, crude protein, calcium, phosphorus, digestible amino acids, vitamin/mineral premixes, and measured feed intake. One breeding study tested complete pelleted diets at 15–18% crude protein and 12.2–12.6 MJ/kg metabolizable energy; a racing-pigeon study evaluated energy in complete diets that also reported calcium, phosphorus, lysine, methionine, and premix composition.[10] [11]

The product implication is that a pigeon seed/grain formula may be presented as a **batch mix estimate**, but it must not imply that matching four macronutrients makes a balanced ration. The product needs micronutrient and metabolizable-energy coverage before using “optimized” or “precision nutrition” as a complete-diet claim.

## Supplements and herbal claims

MSD Veterinary Manual advises against adding supplements to drinking water because they can change taste, decrease water intake, or degrade. It also says that birds eating balanced pellets generally do not need extra vitamins or minerals unless a veterinarian recommends them.[12]

The Association of Avian Veterinarians describes weak, cooled tea made from some bird-safe herbs as an enrichment option. This does not validate disease-prevention claims or fixed therapeutic dosages.[13]

The product implication is that the current herb database should not present unsupported health claims such as “natural antibiotic,” “antiviral,” “immune booster,” “antiparasitic,” or fixed feed/water dosage regimens. The recommended safe action is to downgrade the tab to optional enrichment only, remove water-additive directions, and add a veterinarian-review note for any supplement, medical condition, breeding bird, or medicated bird.
